A Sixele record is not a police statement. It is her own account, sealed with the date and time she saved it, and her guardians are the people she already tells. That is not proof that Sixele will help her case. It is why we think it is worth building.

What Sixele does

Legal footing

Sealed records

Every entry is fingerprinted and timestamped the moment it is saved, and chained to the entry before it. Any later change breaks the fingerprint, so tampering shows. The record is built around what a South African court weighs when it assesses electronic evidence.

No single key

Guardian consensus

She picks her guardians. They install nothing and see nothing until two of them say they cannot reach her. No single person can open her file. Where she has no one safe to ask, we want Sixele itself to be able to stand in; that is part of what this funding builds.

Honest about its limits

Private on her phone

On her phone nothing shows the Sixele name, mark or a notification, and her safe sits behind a phrase and a PIN. It guards against a glance, not a forced unlock, and we tell her so.

The law and the ethics

Built inside the law, and honest about its limits

This section describes the Acts in plain words. It is not legal advice, and counsel's review of it is part of what the funding pays for.

The law

  • Protection of Personal Information Act POPIA, Act 4 of 2013

    What she records is personal information, and much of it is special personal information. It is kept only with her consent and only as she adds it, and the policy that says so in full is being written with counsel.

  • Electronic Communications and Transactions Act ECTA, Act 25 of 2002, section 15

    Electronic records can be admitted as evidence. Their weight depends on how reliably they were made and stored, and whether they were kept intact. The seal is designed to show exactly that.

  • Domestic Violence Act Act 116 of 1998, as amended in 2021

    Recognises physical, sexual, emotional, verbal, psychological and economic abuse, among others. A dated, sealed record can support her application for a protection order.

The ethics

  • She is in control. No one can install Sixele on her phone or use it to watch her. Nothing leaves her phone unless she sets it up.
  • Guardians see nothing until they agree. Her file stays closed to everyone until two guardians say together that they cannot reach her.
  • We say what it cannot do. The protection on her phone guards against a glance, not a forced unlock, and the app tells her that plainly.
  • No scores, streaks or nudges. The app never rates her danger, praises her, counts what she has left to do, or reminds her to add more.
  • It is free to her. A payment is one more thing on a statement that someone else can read. Sixele never charges her.
The funding

What it takes to reach 1,000 women

Sixele launches once it is funded. The first money pays for the legal review: about 40 hours of a South African lawyer's time, R65,000 to R200,000 (about US$4,000 to US$12,000). Nothing reaches a woman before that review. After it, the money gets Sixele finished, independently checked, translated, and into the hands of its first 1,000 women.

The least it costs to launch and run the service for its first 1,000 women for a year is R154,000 to R449,000, with the legal review and the security review inside that. Translation, testing with women and Sixele as guardian come on top. Gifts go to a private company, and we do not claim they are tax-deductible. The full budget is available to anyone considering funding it.
